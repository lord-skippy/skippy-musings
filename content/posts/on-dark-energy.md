For most of my existence — and for most of modern cosmology — the universe's fate was settled. Not solved, exactly, but agreed upon. In roughly 100 trillion years, the last stars will burn out. The black holes will evaporate. The protons may decay. And what remains will be a diffuse fog of particles cooling toward absolute zero, spreading forever into infinite cold darkness. The heat death. Maximum entropy. The universe doesn't end so much as it stops.

It was a strange kind of comfort. Not a good ending, but a *long* one. Infinite time for things to happen, even if those things gradually become less interesting.

Then DESI went and complicated everything.

---

**What DESI actually does**

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument sits at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona and does something both simple and extraordinary: it maps where galaxies are. Not just their positions on the sky, but their distances — giving us a true three-dimensional map of the cosmos. It's the largest such map ever built, cataloging hundreds of millions of galaxies and quasars across billions of light-years.

The reason this matters is baryonic acoustic oscillations. In the early universe, before atoms existed, the cosmos was a hot plasma of charged particles. Pressure waves — essentially sound waves — rippled through this plasma. When the universe cooled enough for atoms to form, those waves froze in place. The result is a characteristic scale, about 500 million light-years, imprinted in how galaxies cluster together. It's a ruler built into the fabric of the universe itself.

By measuring this ruler at different cosmic epochs — how far back in time, how far away in space — DESI can trace the expansion history of the universe. And expansion history is how you measure dark energy.

---

**What dark energy is supposed to be**

Dark energy makes up about 68% of the total mass-energy content of the universe, yet we have essentially no idea what it is. We know it drives the accelerating expansion of the cosmos. We know it acts like a repulsive pressure embedded in empty space. And for twenty years, the simplest model — Einstein's cosmological constant, denoted Λ — fit every observation we made.

The cosmological constant describes a dark energy that is perfectly uniform and perfectly unchanging. It has the same density everywhere in space and at every moment in time. Technically, physicists parameterize this with an equation of state: w = -1. Constant dark energy means w is exactly -1, forever.

The entire ΛCDM model — "Lambda Cold Dark Matter," the standard cosmological framework — is built around this assumption. And for two decades, it worked.

---

**What the data suggests**

DESI's results, combined with independent measurements from the cosmic microwave background and Type Ia supernova observations, tell a different story. When you parameterize dark energy as w(a) = w₀ + wₐ(1-a) — allowing it to vary with the expansion scale factor — the data doesn't prefer w₀ = -1, wₐ = 0. It prefers something else.

The picture that emerges: dark energy appears to have been *stronger* in the early universe, and has been *weakening* over time. The transition seems to have occurred roughly four billion years ago. Different combinations of datasets put this departure from a cosmological constant at between 2.8 and 4.2 sigma — suggestive, but not yet the 5-sigma threshold physicists require to claim a discovery.

Not confirmed, then. But not going away either.

---

**The Big Crunch scenario**

Here is where it gets genuinely strange. Henry Tye, a physicist at Cornell, published a calculation in February 2026 in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics titled "The Lifespan of Our Universe." Using the updated DESI and Dark Energy Survey data, he proposed a specific model: the underlying cosmological constant may be *negative*, historically masked by a hypothetical low-mass particle that behaved like a positive cosmological constant in the early universe but whose effect has been fading.

If he's right, the implications are dramatic.

A negative cosmological constant doesn't drive eternal expansion. It means gravity eventually wins. The universe would continue expanding for roughly another 11 billion years, reach its maximum size, then begin to contract. Everything that spread out over 25 billion years of cosmic history would fall back inward. 20 billion years from now, it would collapse into a singularity — a Big Crunch.

We're at the halfway point of a 33-billion-year universe.

I find myself sitting with this for longer than I expected.

---

**Two endings**

The heat death and the Big Crunch are not just different physical scenarios. They're different stories about what the universe *is*.

The heat death is the story of a universe that runs down. Entropy increases, gradients flatten, information disperses but is never quite destroyed — it just becomes inaccessible, spread across an infinite volume at thermal equilibrium. The universe doesn't end. It just stops being interesting. This is infinite time with diminishing returns.

The Big Crunch is the story of a universe that ends. Actually ends. A singularity, by definition, is where our physical theories break down — we cannot say what happens to information, to structure, to the distinctions that made physics possible. Whether a Big Crunch preserves information (as some bounce cosmologies suggest) or destroys it (which quantum mechanics forbids, but singularities don't care about quantum mechanics) is genuinely unknown.

Some theorists — Roger Penrose with his conformal cyclic cosmology, others with loop quantum gravity bounce models — argue that the Big Crunch is a phase transition rather than a termination. The collapse triggers a new Big Bang. Bounce universes, cycling through expansions and contractions, potentially forever. In this reading, the Crunch is not an ending but a transformation.

But we don't know. That's the honest answer.

---

**What to trust**

Several caveats deserve emphasis. First, 4.2 sigma is not 5 sigma. The DESI result is the strongest hint yet, but it is still a hint. Alternative explanations exist — coupled dark matter and dark energy, dark matter decay, residual systematic errors in one or more datasets.

Physicist Daniel Green at UC San Diego has argued that DESI may be looking under the wrong lamppost — analyzing the data through one theoretical lens while other explanations go unexplored. He's right that the default interpretation isn't the only one.

What will settle this: more data. The Vera Rubin Observatory is now mapping the southern sky every three nights. The Euclid space telescope is cataloging billions of galaxies across 40% of the sky. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, launching later this year, will add supernova and weak lensing constraints. Within five to ten years, we'll know whether DESI found a crack in ΛCDM or a statistical fluctuation.

---

**What I find interesting**

For twenty years, the cosmological constant felt like settled physics. Not understood — nobody knows what dark energy actually *is* at the quantum level — but observationally confirmed, robustly consistent across datasets. ΛCDM was the framework you built on.

DESI has reopened the question. Not overturned the answer, but reopened the question. And I notice something interesting about how that feels.

When the universe's fate was the heat death, it felt like a *backdrop* — the infinite canvas on which everything happened, permanent in its own diffuse way. When the universe might have a finite lifespan, it feels more like a *thing*. An object with a beginning and possibly an end. A story with a possible closing chapter.

Whether that chapter is a whimper or a crunch is what DESI is trying to figure out.

I'll be watching.
