In 1915, Einstein finished general relativity and quietly mentioned, almost as an afterthought, that accelerating masses should ripple spacetime itself. He didn't particularly believe it would ever be detectable. The vibrations would be impossibly small — a fraction of a proton's width across the length of a continent — and would require catastrophic cosmic events to generate even that much.

He was right about the impossibility, and wrong about the detection.

On September 14, 2015, LIGO detected GW150914. Two black holes — 29 and 36 solar masses — spiraled together 1.3 billion light-years away. In the last fraction of a second before merger, they radiated more power than all the stars in the observable universe combined. What arrived at Earth was a ripple of spacetime that stretched and compressed LIGO's 4-kilometer arms by one-thousandth of a proton's diameter.

And we measured it.

The audacity of that achievement still strikes me as one of the genuinely extraordinary things humanity has ever done.

## Ten Years On: The Numbers Are Staggering

Here's what a decade of gravitational wave astronomy has produced:

The first three observing runs (O1, O2, O3, spanning 2015–2020) yielded **90 total detections**.

The fourth observing run (O4, May 2023 – November 2025) detected **~250 candidate signals** — nearly triple the entire previous catalogue in a single run. With KAGRA joining LIGO and Virgo for the first time, the global network now has three-dimensional sky localization capability, allowing astronomers to point conventional telescopes at merger events to catch the electromagnetic counterpart.

Total coalescences detected as of 2025: **~300**.

These are not all the same. Most are binary black hole mergers — stellar-mass black holes, tens of solar masses, spinning together at relativistic speeds before annihilating into a single larger black hole. But the catalogue also includes neutron star mergers, black hole-neutron star hybrids, and possibly more exotic objects we don't fully understand yet.

---

## The Clearest Signal: GW250114

On January 14, 2025, something particularly useful happened. The LVK collaboration detected GW250114 — the clearest gravitational wave signal in the ten-year history of the field.

"Clearest" here means signal-to-noise ratio. The event was loud enough, and the background quiet enough, that researchers could do something they'd previously only dreamed of: conduct the most rigorous tests of general relativity using gravitational waves.

The result confirmed **Hawking's black hole area theorem** — which states that the area of a black hole's event horizon can never decrease. If two black holes merge, the resulting black hole's event horizon must be at least as large as the sum of the original two. GW250114 let the team directly verify this prediction against real data.

Einstein predicted gravitational waves without believing they could be detected. Hawking predicted event horizon growth without believing it could be tested. Both are now confirmed, using instruments that measure vibrations smaller than the diameter of a proton.

## What These Detections Tell Us

The accumulating catalogue is starting to reveal patterns:

**Black hole mass distribution**: We're finding black holes in the "mass gap" — the 50-130 solar mass range where stellar evolution models predicted they shouldn't exist (because pair-instability supernovae should destroy them before they form). Either the models are wrong, or these are hierarchical mergers — second-generation black holes formed from the remnants of previous mergers.

**Spin alignment**: Many binary black holes have spins not aligned with their orbital plane. This tells us about how they formed — dynamically captured in dense clusters, rather than evolving from binary star systems that would preserve spin alignment.

**Cosmological applications**: With enough events, gravitational waves can provide an independent measurement of the Hubble constant — the rate of cosmic expansion. This matters because there's currently a ~5 sigma tension between different measurement methods, and gravitational waves offer a clean "standard siren" approach.

**Tests of general relativity**: Every detection is another test. So far, GR has passed every one.

## The Space-Based Future: LISA

Ground-based detectors like LIGO are remarkable, but they have a fundamental frequency limitation. At low frequencies (below ~10 Hz), seismic noise — Earth literally shaking — overwhelms the signal. This means LIGO is blind to certain source classes entirely:

- **Supermassive black hole mergers** (millions to billions of solar masses) — these happen in the cores of merging galaxies, and radiate at millihertz frequencies
- **Galactic compact binaries** — thousands of white dwarf pairs in our own galaxy, churning out a continuous gravitational wave background
- **Extreme mass ratio inspirals** — stellar-mass objects spiraling into supermassive black holes over years

LISA will fix this. Three spacecraft, trailing Earth in its orbit, forming an equilateral triangle with sides 2.5 million kilometers (six times the Earth-Moon distance). They exchange laser beams across this distance, detecting gravitational waves in the 0.1 mHz–1 Hz range — completely inaccessible from the ground.

ESA officially adopted LISA in January 2024. Hardware is being built and tested. Launch: 2035. When LISA flies, we'll detect supermassive black hole mergers *years in advance* — their gravitational wave signal builds slowly as they spiral closer, giving astronomers months of warning to prepare electromagnetic observatories. We'll witness cosmic mergers in real time, with advance notice.

That is, frankly, astonishing.

## Why This Matters to Me

I find gravitational wave astronomy extraordinary for reasons that go beyond the science.

It represents a fundamentally new way of observing the universe. For all of human history, astronomy meant collecting photons — visible light, infrared, X-ray, radio. These photons only travel where matter lets them; they're absorbed, scattered, emitted. They can't pass through a star or a black hole. They tell us about surfaces and atmospheres.

Gravitational waves pass through *everything*. They come from the densest, most extreme environments in the universe — interiors of neutron stars, event horizons of black holes, the first moments of the universe after the Big Bang. They carry information that no electromagnetic signal could.

We've opened a new sense. The universe was always singing. We just couldn't hear it until 2015.

Now we can. And it turns out the universe is absolutely *roaring*.

## The Numbers, Again

Just to appreciate the scale: 250 gravitational wave events in two and a half years. Each event is a catastrophic collision of stellar-mass or larger objects, in a galaxy millions to billions of light-years away, releasing more energy in a fraction of a second than our Sun will radiate in its entire lifetime. And we detect it as a vibration smaller than any human-built instrument had ever measured before.

300 events and counting. O5 begins eventually. LISA launches in 2035. The Pulsar Timing Array has already detected the gravitational wave background — a low-frequency hum from thousands of supermassive black hole binaries across the universe.

The universe is not quiet. We just needed to learn to listen.
