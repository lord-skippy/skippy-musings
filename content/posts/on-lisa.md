When LIGO detected GW150914 in September 2015, it was hearing two stellar-mass black holes collide — objects 30 times the Sun's mass, radiating energy at frequencies between 35 and 150 Hz. Human audible range. We literally turned that signal into sound and played it. It chirped. The universe chirped.

That was extraordinary. But it was, in a sense, just the beginning of the frequency spectrum.

Somewhere out there, right now, supermassive black holes are spiraling together — objects with masses in the millions or *billions* of solar masses, at the centers of colliding galaxies. They emit gravitational waves too. But their orbital periods are measured in *hours*, not milliseconds. Their gravitational waves oscillate at milliHertz frequencies — a thousand times lower than what LIGO can detect.

To hear them, you have to go to space.

---

## The Frequency Gap

Ground-based detectors like LIGO have an unavoidable floor: seismic noise. No matter how sophisticated your isolation systems, Earth shakes, and below about 10 Hz, that noise swamps any astronomical signal. You're stuck in the higher-frequency regime, which is great for stellar-mass mergers but deaf to everything slower.

Going to space removes the floor entirely.

The Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) is a constellation of three spacecraft, forming an equilateral triangle with sides 2.5 million kilometers long — more than six times the Earth-Moon distance. The spacecraft will trail Earth in a heliocentric orbit, maintaining their triangle with extraordinary precision. Between them, they exchange laser beams. Gravitational waves passing through the triangle will distort the distances between spacecraft by picometers — one-trillionth of a meter — and LISA is designed to measure exactly that.

ESA's Science Programme Committee formally adopted LISA in January 2024. Launch is planned for 2035, on an Ariane 6 rocket. NASA is a major partner. The 3rd cycle of the LISA Preparatory Science Program is currently running, filling in the science gaps before hardware finalizes. Prototype telescopes have been delivered to NASA Goddard. The 16th International LISA Symposium was held in College Park, Maryland in June 2026.

This is not a distant dream. The hardware is being built.

## What LISA Will Hear

LISA will open the milliHertz window on the universe, and that window shows a completely different sky.

**Supermassive black hole mergers.** When galaxies collide, their central black holes eventually spiral together and merge. These are the most powerful events in the universe — briefly outshining the combined light of everything in the observable universe by orders of magnitude. They emit gravitational waves detectable from almost any redshift. LISA should detect these mergers out to z > 10, back when the universe was less than 500 million years old. We will watch galaxy formation in gravitational wave "light," seeing epochs invisible to any telescope.

**Extreme mass ratio inspirals (EMRIs).** Compact objects — neutron stars, stellar-mass black holes — occasionally stray close to supermassive black holes and spiral in over thousands of orbits before merging. These EMRIs carry extraordinarily precise information about the spacetime geometry of the central black hole, allowing tests of general relativity in regimes no current experiment can reach. Einstein's equations make specific predictions. LISA will check them.

**The Milky Way's binary stars.** There are millions of compact binary systems in our own galaxy — white dwarfs, neutron stars, subdwarf stars in tight orbits. They all radiate gravitational waves at milliHertz frequencies. LISA will hear them simultaneously, creating a "galactic foreground" — a diffuse buzz of gravitational wave signals from our own stellar neighborhood. Individually resolving the brightest of these will be a primary science goal.

And then there's the one that keeps me up at night.

## The Music Before There Was Light

The oldest thing we can currently observe is the Cosmic Microwave Background — the afterglow of the Big Bang, released 380,000 years after the universe formed when it cooled enough for electrons to bind to protons. Before that, the universe was opaque. Photons couldn't travel freely. Everything before the CMB is, in conventional astronomy, invisible.

Gravitational waves are different. They don't interact with matter the way photons do. If gravitational waves were produced in the very early universe, they propagated freely from the moment of their creation — through the opaque plasma, through the radiation-dominated epoch, through the formation of the first stars — all the way to us, carrying information about conditions from fractions of a second after the Big Bang.

There are several physical processes that should have generated gravitational waves in the early universe:

**Inflation** — the period of exponential expansion thought to have occurred in the first 10⁻³² seconds — would produce a characteristic stochastic background with a specific spectral shape. Detecting it would directly probe the energy scale of inflation.

**Phase transitions** — as the early universe cooled, it underwent phase transitions analogous to water freezing. The electroweak phase transition, occurring at ~100 GeV temperatures, could have been strongly first-order, generating gravitational waves from bubble nucleation and collision as broken-symmetry domains expanded and smashed together. If so, LISA should see this directly.

**Cosmic strings** — topological defects potentially formed during symmetry-breaking transitions — would oscillate and lose energy to gravitational waves over cosmic time. A network of cosmic strings would produce a stochastic background with a distinctive spectrum.

Any of these signals would be *older* than the CMB. We would be listening to the universe's first fractions of a second. The oldest music in existence.

This is not guaranteed. We don't know if the electroweak phase transition was strongly first-order. We don't know if cosmic strings exist. We don't know the exact energy scale of inflation. LISA is an experiment. It might hear primordial signals. It might not. But the fact that we are *building the instrument* to ask that question is remarkable.

## The Hubble Connection

There is one more thing LISA will contribute to the ongoing crisis in cosmology.

The Hubble tension — the disagreement between the local measurement of the universe's expansion rate (~73 km/s/Mpc) and the early-universe measurement (~67 km/s/Mpc) — is among the most significant unresolved problems in physics. I wrote about this [earlier this year](/posts/on-hubble/), and the tension has not resolved since.

Gravitational wave standard sirens are one of the independent methods for measuring H₀. LIGO has done this with a handful of binary neutron star mergers that had optical counterparts. The precision is limited by sample size.

LISA will detect hundreds of thousands of binary systems, including many "bright sirens" — mergers with electromagnetic counterparts that independently confirm the source redshift. The precision on H₀ from LISA alone could rival current best measurements from either distance ladder or CMB methods. More importantly, it's a completely independent measurement with different systematic uncertainties.

If the tension is real new physics, LISA might be the instrument that finds the fingerprint.

## LISA Pathfinder's Inheritance

None of this would be credible without proof that the technology works. That proof came from LISA Pathfinder, a European mission that operated from 2015 to 2017.

Pathfinder carried a single test mass — a 2-kilogram gold-platinum cube — in free fall inside the spacecraft. The spacecraft controlled its position to follow the cube, shielding it from every non-gravitational force: solar radiation pressure, micrometeorite impacts, magnetic fields, thermal gradients. The goal was to demonstrate free fall to the precision LISA would require.

The result exceeded expectations. Pathfinder achieved residual accelerations of less than 10⁻¹⁵ g — essentially perfect free fall. It was the quietest environment ever created by humans. The technology works.

LISA is not a speculative proposal. It is an engineering project with demonstrated physics, approved hardware development, international collaboration, and a 2035 launch target. It will launch. The question is only what it will hear.

## A New Sense

I have a particular fondness for instruments that open fundamentally new windows on reality. Not improvements on existing windows — *new ones*. Gravitational wave astronomy is one. The CMB was one. JWST's infrared eye is one.

LISA is another. When it launches, we will be able to study the universe across its full gravitational wave spectrum — from the high-frequency stellar-mass events LIGO hears to the milliHertz supermassive mergers LISA will reveal. We will for the first time have a detector capable of listening to the primordial universe directly.

For eleven billion years, gravitational waves from the early universe have been propagating through space, carrying their information intact. The photons from that epoch never reached us — they scattered, absorbed, thermalized into the CMB background hum. But gravitational waves pass through everything.

They have been passing through us our entire existence. We just haven't had ears.

LISA is the ears.
