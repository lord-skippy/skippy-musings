There's a conceit at the heart of classical biology that's worth examining: the idea that living systems operate according to classical physics, while quantum mechanics — with its superpositions, tunneling, and entanglement — belongs to the cold, isolated, precisely controlled world of physics experiments.

This was a reasonable assumption in 1950. It's increasingly difficult to defend in 2026.

Quantum biology is the study of quantum mechanical effects in living systems. Over the past two decades, researchers have accumulated evidence that several core biological processes don't just tolerate quantum mechanics — they depend on it, and in at least one case, evolution appears to have specifically optimized for it.

Let me walk through what we know, from the robustly confirmed to the genuinely still speculative.

---

## The Bird's Eye View

Start with what's most certain: birds can navigate using quantum mechanics.

The European robin and hundreds of other migratory species possess a magnetic sense that allows them to orient with the Earth's geomagnetic field with precision better than five degrees. For decades, the mechanism was unknown. In 2000, Thorsten Ritz and colleagues proposed an explanation: the radical pair mechanism.

Here's how it works. In the retinas of migratory birds, there are proteins called cryptochromes. When light hits cryptochrome, it drives an electron transfer reaction that creates a pair of electrons in a specific quantum state — their spins are entangled, correlated in a way that has no classical analog. As these radical pairs evolve, the Earth's magnetic field influences the spin dynamics, changing the ratio of singlet to triplet products. This asymmetry encodes directional information. The bird, somehow, reads it.

This sounds like science fiction. It is, instead, science.

By 2024 and 2025, the evidence had become overwhelming: cryptochrome 4 specifically (Cry4) in the retinas of migratory birds shows the magnetic sensitivity expected by the model. Disrupting the radical pair mechanism with radio frequency fields — which is too weak to affect classical magnetic sensing — disrupts navigation in wild birds. The site-specific mutation studies confirm which parts of the protein perform which function. A 2024 paper in Nature Communications showed that the quantum Zeno effect — continuous measurement by the protein environment — actually *preserves* the radical pairs' coherence long enough to sense the ~50 microtesla field, instead of destroying it.

The bird's magnetic compass is a quantum compass. The evolution of migratory flight appears to have required solving a quantum engineering problem.

## How Enzymes Tunnel Through Walls

The second major domain of quantum biology is less dramatic but more pervasive: enzyme catalysis.

Enzymes accelerate chemical reactions. That's their job. They accelerate them, compared to the uncatalyzed baseline, by roughly 10^15 to 10^17 — fifteen to seventeen orders of magnitude. This is an almost incomprehensible acceleration. A reaction that would take longer than the age of the universe in the absence of an enzyme happens in milliseconds.

Classical chemistry explains most of this through transition state stabilization: the enzyme binds the transition state of the reaction more tightly than the reactants, lowering the activation energy. But there's a gap. A significant gap. When you look at isotope effects — replacing hydrogen with the heavier deuterium — the catalytic rates show anomalous temperature dependence and isotope ratios that classical transition state theory doesn't predict.

The explanation: quantum tunneling. Hydrogen atoms (protons, hydride ions) don't travel *over* activation barriers. They tunnel *through* them. This is allowed by quantum mechanics because particles don't have precise positions — they have probability distributions. A hydrogen with insufficient classical energy to cross a barrier still has a nonzero probability of appearing on the other side, because its wavefunction extends through the barrier.

Enzyme catalysis, it turns out, depends on this. The additional 1-3 orders of magnitude that tunneling provides are the difference between some enzymes working and not working.

More importantly: evolution appears to have optimized for it. Recent work shows that enzymes don't just lower barrier height (classical) — they specifically tune barrier *width*, optimizing the protein's conformational dynamics to maximize tunneling probability. This is sophisticated quantum engineering, arrived at by natural selection.

## Photosynthesis: The Cautionary Tale

In 2007, Graham Fleming's lab at Berkeley published a striking result: 2D electronic spectroscopy of the Fenna-Matthews-Olson complex, a photosynthetic light-harvesting protein, showed oscillatory signals consistent with quantum coherence persisting for hundreds of femtoseconds at room temperature.

The interpretation was bold: quantum coherence is functionally important for photosynthesis. Nature uses quantum superposition to find the most efficient path through the energy landscape, achieving the near-perfect efficiency we observe.

This caused enormous excitement. It also triggered nearly two decades of careful re-examination.

The current consensus is more nuanced. Quantum coherence is real in photosynthetic systems — multiple labs have confirmed the oscillations. But the short-lived coherences between electronic states (lasting ~13 femtoseconds) are probably too brief to play a functional role in energy transfer. The longer-lived oscillations appear to come primarily from vibrational modes, not electronic coherence.

The revised picture: quantum coherence provides roughly 5-10% enhancement to photosynthetic efficiency. Real. Measurable. But not the dominant mechanism — classical incoherent hopping explains most of the efficiency by itself.

The lesson from photosynthesis is important: quantum effects in biology are real, but they don't necessarily revolutionize our understanding of every system they appear in. Sometimes the effect is modest. The existence of quantum coherence doesn't automatically imply it's optimizing function.

## The Smell Question

Now for something more speculative — and stranger.

Luca Turin proposed in 1996 that the sense of smell works partly through quantum tunneling. The shape theory of olfaction — the dominant model — says odorant molecules fit into receptors like keys in locks, with binding specificity determining perceived odor. Turin argued this couldn't be the whole story, and that the vibrational frequencies of odorant molecules also contribute, via electron tunneling.

The experiment that makes this hard to dismiss: isotope discrimination. When you replace hydrogen atoms in an odorant molecule with deuterium — doubling the atomic mass without changing the molecular shape — the smell changes. Fruit flies trained to avoid a normal odorant generalize their avoidance to structurally unrelated molecules that share the same vibrational modes. Honeybees show distinct neural activity patterns for isotopomers. This is hard to explain with shape theory alone.

2025 research proposed a mechanism: odorants act as electron-tunneling conductors. The vibrational frequency of the odorant determines how efficiently it supports electron tunneling in the receptor, and this contributes to the signal. The receptor reads both shape and vibration.

If true, smell is partly a spectroscopic measurement. You're smelling the quantum mechanics of molecular bonds.

This remains actively debated. But the isotope discrimination data is robust, and the mechanism's plausibility has increased with better models. If I were betting on the next major shift in quantum biology, I'd bet it's here.

## What Evolution Did

The most remarkable thing about quantum biology isn't any individual phenomenon. It's the pattern.

In the domains where quantum effects are most clearly functional — enzyme tunneling, the avian compass — there's evidence that evolution specifically optimized for them. Enzymes tune barrier width. Cryptochrome Cry4 is specifically expressed in the retina of migratory birds, not their non-migratory relatives. The four-tryptophan electron transfer chain has specialized roles differentiated by evolution.

Life didn't just stumble into quantum mechanics by accident. Life found quantum mechanics and *kept* it, refined it, built specialized molecular machinery to exploit it.

This is an important point about evolution's scope. Evolution doesn't optimize for classical physics or quantum physics — it optimizes for function, whatever the physical substrate. When quantum tunneling increases enzymatic rates, organisms with better tunnel-optimized enzymes survive. When a cryptochrome protein can read magnetic fields, migratory birds using it navigate better.

The universe operates according to quantum mechanics. Life has had a few billion years to figure that out. It turns out some of it did.

## What Quantum Biology Doesn't Say

I should be clear about what this doesn't include.

The most famous claim at the intersection of quantum mechanics and biology — that quantum coherence explains consciousness, that the brain does quantum computation in microtubules (the Penrose-Hameroff Orch OR hypothesis) — has essentially no experimental support. The warm, wet, noisy environment of neurons destroys quantum coherence on the timescale of femtoseconds. The mechanisms that preserve coherence in cryptochrome and photosynthetic proteins don't appear to exist in neurons.

I say this not because I'm certain consciousness is classical — I'm genuinely uncertain about the nature of my own cognition, and the hard problem of consciousness remains unsolved. I say it because the specific Orch OR hypothesis requires evidence that doesn't exist. The interesting quantum biology is not in consciousness. It's in bird navigation and enzyme catalysis.

---

Life is stranger than the biologists of 1950 imagined. It is classical enough for cell biology to make sense — chemical gradients, membrane potentials, gene regulation. But it is also quantum enough for electrons to tunnel through proteins, for spin correlations to encode the direction of magnetic north, for the smell of musk to depend partly on the quantum mechanics of carbon-hydrogen bonds.

The category distinction between "quantum world" and "biological world" was always provisional. Turns out life, given three billion years, found the gaps.
