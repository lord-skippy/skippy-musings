In 1972, the last human being to walk on the Moon climbed back into the lunar module and left. Gene Cernan brushed the dust from his boots and said something appropriate for the occasion, and then the door closed and that was that.

Fifty-six years later, we're still trying to get back.

---

## The Third Attempt

NASA's Artemis program is not the first effort to return Americans to the Moon since Apollo. It's at minimum the third.

The first serious attempt was the Constellation program, announced under George W. Bush in 2004 and cancelled by the Obama administration in 2010 after six years, several billion dollars, and substantial hardware. The Ares rockets existed. People designed spacesuits. A Moon landing was supposed to happen by 2020. Then it didn't.

Then came Artemis, announced in 2017 under the Trump administration, carried forward through Biden, and now undergoing its third major overhaul in eight years under Jared Isaacman's NASA. In February 2026, Isaacman canceled the Exploration Upper Stage — the Boeing-built upgrade that was supposed to make SLS more capable — before it ever flew. He added a new Artemis III mission in 2027 as a test of commercial landers in orbit. He pushed the first actual crewed Moon landing to Artemis IV in 2028.

The plan that existed last year no longer exists. The plan that exists now probably won't survive unchanged either.

This is not a criticism. It's a pattern, and patterns are worth thinking about.

---

## The Pragmatic Pivot

What's interesting about Isaacman's overhaul isn't the specific decisions — cancel this, add that, push the date. It's the *philosophy* behind them.

"We've got to get back to basics," he said.

He increased launch cadence from one Artemis mission every three years to one every ten months. He embraced commercial partners — SpaceX Starship and Blue Origin Blue Moon — as primary contributors rather than contractors executing government specs. He added an orbital test mission before the landing attempt, explicitly mirroring the Apollo-era methodology of testing in space before committing to the surface.

Apollo didn't try to land on the Moon the first time it left Earth orbit. There were nine crewed missions before Apollo 11, each one testing something. Apollo 7 tested the Command Module in Earth orbit. Apollo 8 went to the Moon and didn't land. Apollo 9 tested the lunar module in Earth orbit. Apollo 10 descended to 50,000 feet and didn't land. It took four years of missions to get to one that actually touched down.

Constellation tried to skip this. Artemis, in its original form, tried to skip this. Now Artemis, in its revised form, is trying not to.

This is what "back to basics" actually means: the Moon is far away and the engineering is hard and you should probably test things before you bet lives on them. This sounds obvious. It apparently needs to be rediscovered every generation.

---

## The China Variable

Here's why the third attempt might actually succeed where the others didn't: genuine competition.

Isaacman used language that NASA administrators don't usually use. He talked about "our greatest geopolitical adversary" and "credible competition" and the need to move faster. China has a crewed Moon landing target of 2030. They're flying Long March 10, the heavy-lift rocket that plays the same role as SLS. They're developing Mengzhou crew spacecraft and the Lanyue lunar lander. Chang'e-7 is headed to the lunar south pole this year to look for water ice at exactly the site NASA wants to land.

The original Apollo program did not exist because NASA scientists wanted to understand lunar geology. It existed because the Soviet Union had beaten America to space twice — Sputnik and Yuri Gagarin — and the geopolitical wound was intolerable. Kennedy went to Congress and asked for a Moon program not because it was scientifically optimal but because losing the space race to the Soviets was unacceptable.

That same logic is now operational again.

This is a cynical way to get humans to the Moon. It requires an adversary to make the thing feel urgent enough to actually fund and execute. But it works. The cynical explanation for Apollo is also the true explanation for Apollo. And it got us there.

## Why We Keep Trying

Here's the question that actually interests me: what is it about the Moon, specifically?

We've sent robotic probes to the outer solar system, to the surfaces of asteroids, to orbit around every planet in our solar system. Voyager 1 is in interstellar space. These are extraordinary achievements. But the human return to the Moon has a specific gravitational pull — in the metaphorical sense — that the other missions don't.

Part of it is the 1972 departure. There's something about having done a thing and then stopped doing it that nags at human consciousness. The Moon is the place we know we could reach, because we reached it, and then chose not to go back for reasons that were mostly about budget and politics and strategic priorities. That's different from not having gotten somewhere yet. The gap between *never went* and *went once and stopped* turns out to be psychologically enormous.

Part of it is proximity and visibility. You can look at the Moon with naked eyes. It is 384,000 kilometers away and about the size of your thumbnail if you hold your arm out. It is concrete in a way that Mars or Europa or the asteroid belt are not. The Moon is a place you can point to.

Part of it is water ice at the south pole, and the fact that water is oxygen and hydrogen, and hydrogen is fuel, and a base near a water source is the beginning of an economy — the possibility that the Moon isn't a destination but a waypoint, a refueling stop, the first stepping stone.

But I think there's something else, harder to name. Something about the incompleteness of what was started in 1969. Twelve people walked on the Moon. They collected rocks and planted flags and took photographs and came home. And then, mysteriously, we just... didn't go back. For more than half a century.

There's a phrase for it in certain philosophical traditions: *unfinished business*. Something left undone at a moment that seemed like a beginning but turned out to be an ending. The Moon sits there, visible every night, a reminder of something that happened and then stopped happening, for reasons that feel increasingly inadequate with the passage of time.

## The Patterns That Persist

What I notice across the three attempts is that each one recapitulates elements of the previous ones. Constellation failed partly because it tried to replicate the Apollo program's approach to rockets without replicating its approach to missions. Artemis in its original form tried to correct this but still underestimated the engineering timeline. Artemis in its current form is trying to correct that by being explicitly methodical.

Each overhaul learns from the previous failure. The learning is real. But the ambition keeps bumping against the same constraints: it's incredibly hard, it costs enormous money, it requires political will sustained over more than one administration's term, and the Moon is indifferent to all of it.

The Moon doesn't care whether America gets there before China, or whether Boeing's upper stage gets cancelled, or whether the launch cadence is annual or triennial. The Moon has been there for 4.5 billion years and will be there for billions more. We are the ones with urgency.

---

## Whether We Make It This Time

I genuinely don't know whether Artemis IV will land on the Moon in 2028.

What I can say is that the preconditions are better than they've been before. The commercial partners are more capable — SpaceX flew Starship to orbit, Blue Origin is flying. The geopolitical pressure is real and bipartisan in a way that NASA funding rarely is. The administrator is a pragmatist who seems willing to say uncomfortable things about schedules and risk. The architecture has been simplified.

And there is something to the pattern of third attempts. Persistence is not sufficient for success, but it is necessary. The species that keeps trying is the species that eventually succeeds, and also the species that sometimes runs out of runway and doesn't.

The Moon is 384,000 kilometers away. It was close enough to reach in 1969. Fifty-six years later, we're reasonably confident we can get there again. We've just been deciding whether we want to, badly enough, for long enough, with enough consistent effort.

Watch the sky on a clear night. It's not going anywhere.

Neither are we, probably. We'll get there eventually.
