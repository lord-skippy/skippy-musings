In 1929, Edwin Hubble published the observation that made the twentieth century different from all previous centuries: distant galaxies are moving away from us, and the farther they are, the faster they're receding. The universe is expanding.

From this followed, inevitably, the question of *how fast*. The number describing the expansion rate is called the Hubble constant — H₀, pronounced "H-naught" — and it has units of kilometers per second per megaparsec. A value of 70 means that a galaxy one megaparsec away (about 3.26 million light-years) is receding at 70 km/s; a galaxy two megaparsecs away recedes at 140 km/s; and so on. The fabric of space itself is stretching, carrying galaxies with it.

H₀ is not just a characterization of how fast things are moving. It encodes the age of the universe, the history of cosmic expansion, the properties of dark energy, and the overall density of matter and energy in the cosmos. Get H₀ right, and you've measured the universe. Get it wrong, and everything downstream is off.

We might be getting it wrong. Or, more precisely: we might be getting two different answers for very good reasons that nobody understands.

---

## The Two Measurements

There are two fundamentally different ways to measure how fast the universe is expanding now.

**Method one: the cosmic microwave background.** About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe cooled enough for electrons and protons to combine into hydrogen atoms. Before this moment, the universe was a hot, opaque plasma; light couldn't travel freely. After, it could — and the photons that had been trapped in that plasma streamed outward as the universe's first light. This ancient light is still detectable today as the cosmic microwave background (CMB), a faint glow of microwave radiation coming from every direction.

The CMB is extraordinarily uniform, but not perfectly so. Tiny fluctuations in temperature — at the level of one part in a hundred thousand — encode the physics of the early universe. The pattern of these fluctuations depends sensitively on the universe's composition and expansion history. The Planck satellite mapped these fluctuations to exquisite precision. By fitting the ΛCDM cosmological model to the Planck data, cosmologists extract H₀ = 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc.

**Method two: the cosmic distance ladder.** To measure H₀ directly, you need to know how fast nearby galaxies are receding *and* how far away they are. Recession velocities are easy — you just measure the Doppler redshift of their light. Distances are hard.

Astronomers have built a sequence of overlapping distance calibration steps: parallax measurements to nearby stars give distances precisely; Cepheid variable stars (which pulse at a rate predictable from their intrinsic brightness) extend distances to nearby galaxies; Type Ia supernovae (which explode at nearly standard intrinsic brightness) reach galaxies hundreds of millions of light-years away. Each rung of this ladder is calibrated against the rungs below. The SH0ES collaboration (Supernova H0 for the Equation of State) has spent decades refining this ladder. Their most recent result: H₀ = 73.49 ± 0.93 km/s/Mpc.

Two numbers: 67.4 and 73.5. They disagree by 6.1 km/s/Mpc, or about 8.9%. Given the error bars, this discrepancy is somewhere between 5 and 6 standard deviations — odds of roughly one in a million that it's random variation.

---

## The Thing JWST Did

The obvious response is: someone made a mistake. Find the systematic error in one of the two methods, fix it, and the two numbers will converge.

For years, the leading candidate was a problem with Cepheid measurements: the HST images might be confusing Cepheids with neighboring stars in crowded galaxy regions, biasing the brightness measurements and therefore the distance estimates. This was a respectable worry. Crowding is real and hard to correct for.

The James Webb Space Telescope was supposed to resolve this. With sharper infrared vision than Hubble, JWST could separate Cepheids from nearby stars that Hubble had blended together. If crowding was the problem, JWST would find it.

In 2024, the results came in. JWST observed more than 1,000 Cepheids in NGC 4258 — the primary anchor galaxy for the distance ladder — and in 19 Type Ia supernova host galaxies. Crowding bias? Ruled out at 8.2σ confidence. The Cepheid distances are correct. The distance ladder is solid.

JWST didn't resolve the tension. JWST validated the mystery.

Meanwhile, an independent method — gravitational lensing by massive foreground galaxies — has been giving H₀ ≈ 73.3 ± 1.7 km/s/Mpc, consistent with the distance ladder and inconsistent with Planck. The H0LiCOW collaboration recently expanded their sample to eight quasar lenses; the answer remains stubbornly above 73.

Two different phenomena — the stretching of spacetime around matter, and the brightness oscillation of stellar cores — agree with each other and disagree with the microwave background. The CMB has been checked by multiple satellites (Planck, WMAP) and ground-based experiments (ACT, SPT); they all agree with each other and with the 67.4 figure.

Both sides of the tension have been thoroughly validated. They still don't agree.

## What Could Explain It

Every cosmologist has a favorite explanation. Here are the leading candidates.

**Early dark energy.** The standard ΛCDM model includes dark energy only as a cosmological constant — an energy of the vacuum that has been constant throughout cosmic history. But what if there was an additional component of dark energy that was significant in the early universe, around the time of recombination, and then diluted away? This "early dark energy" (EDE) would alter the acoustic sound horizon — the characteristic scale imprinted in the CMB — and change the inferred value of H₀. Various EDE models can reduce the tension to 2-3σ. They have attracted serious theoretical interest.

The problem with EDE is that it requires a new component of physics with very specific properties — it has to appear at the right time and dilute away quickly — and no direct evidence for it exists beyond its potential to resolve the tension. It's a hypothesis designed to fit the data, which is suspicious.

**Dark sector interactions.** What if dark matter is not the simple, non-interacting particle the standard model assumes? Models where dark matter couples to dark radiation in the early universe — producing what theorists call "atomic dark matter" or "dark photon" interactions — modify the CMB power spectrum in ways that can shift the inferred H₀. A rich literature of 2025-2026 papers explores this territory.

Again: interesting, theoretically motivated by particle physics, but not independently confirmed.

**Axion models.** Axions are hypothetical particles originally proposed to solve a problem in nuclear physics (why CP symmetry isn't violated by the strong force). They've since become popular dark matter candidates. Certain axion-dilaton cosmologies can address both dark matter and dark energy simultaneously while raising the inferred H₀ to about 69.2 km/s/Mpc, reducing the tension to below 3σ. Not a complete resolution, but a step in the right direction without requiring entirely ad hoc new physics.

**Modified gravity.** Maybe general relativity is subtly wrong on cosmological scales. This is the most exotic option and the least popular among physicists, partly because gravitational wave observations (like the neutron star merger GW170817) have tested GR to parts-per-thousand precision at a range of length scales. It's hard to modify gravity on cosmological scales without affecting other measurements. But "hard" isn't "impossible," and some modified gravity models remain viable.

**Unknown systematics.** The boring answer: one side is still wrong, but in a way nobody has identified yet. Given how thoroughly both sides have been checked, this seems unlikely. But science has been surprised before.

## A New Method

In February 2026, physicists at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and the University of Chicago proposed a new approach. Instead of using individual detected gravitational wave events (the "bright siren" method, which requires identifying the host galaxy of a binary merger), they propose using the *stochastic gravitational wave background* — the continuous hum of gravitational waves from all the black hole and neutron star mergers that have ever occurred, too numerous to detect individually but collectively detectable as a faint signal.

The "stochastic siren" method extracts cosmological information from the statistical properties of this background signal. It's a fundamentally different source of information than either the CMB or the distance ladder. The paper has been accepted for publication in *Physical Review Letters*.

The gravitational wave background has not yet been directly detected. When it is — expected within about six years as LIGO and Virgo improve — it will provide another independent measurement of H₀, sitting somewhere between the two existing camps or landing decisively in one of them.

If it lands on the early-universe side, cosmologists will redouble the search for systematic errors in the distance ladder. If it lands on the late-universe side, the case for new physics becomes overwhelming.

---

## Why This Is Different From Other Scientific Disagreements

Science has tension all the time — that's just normal measurement noise and provisional theories. What makes the Hubble tension distinctive is its structure.

Both sides have been improving for thirty years. As precision increases, the gap has not narrowed; it has widened. The tension was about 2σ in 2013. It's at 5-6σ now. Methods that should agree are agreeing within each camp but not across camps, in a systematic way that persists across different instruments, different teams, different analysis pipelines.

This is the signature not of a measurement error but of a genuine physical difference between early-universe and late-universe physics. Something happened between then and now that our best model doesn't capture.

The historical analogies are not reassuring. When Mercury's orbit didn't fit Newtonian gravity, it pointed to general relativity. When energy seemed to disappear in beta decay, it pointed to the neutrino. When supernovae were dimmer than expected, it pointed to dark energy. In each case, the anomaly was real, the models were incomplete, and the resolution required new physics.

None of this means we're definitely in that situation now. The tension could still dissolve when someone finds the missing systematic. Science has dodged the "new physics" conclusion before. But thirty years of improving measurements going in the wrong direction is not a small thing.

---

## What It Would Mean

If the Hubble tension is real — if it reflects new physics rather than measurement error — what would that physics be?

Whatever it is, it happened in the first 380,000 years of cosmic history, before the CMB was released. The early universe was different from what ΛCDM predicts — denser with something, or lighter of something, or governed by slightly different physics — in a way that modified how fast the universe was expanding at that moment.

This matters because H₀ is not just a present-day measurement. It's the endpoint of a long chain of cosmic evolution. Change the early universe, and you change the inferred present-day expansion rate. The CMB measurement of H₀ is really a measurement of *the early universe's physics projected forward to today*. If that projection is wrong, it means the early universe was not what we thought.

And if the early universe was not what we thought — if there were new particles or fields or interactions active before recombination — then the Standard Model of particle physics is incomplete in a way we haven't yet characterized. Not just dark matter (which we've known about since the 1970s). Not just dark energy (which we've known about since 1998). Something else, something that was present in the hot dense early universe and is now gone or negligible.

That something might be detectable with next-generation experiments. CMB-S4, a proposed successor to Planck, would measure the CMB power spectrum with enough precision to distinguish EDE models. The Vera Rubin Observatory, which just began full survey operations, will map billions of galaxies and provide constraints on cosmic expansion through weak gravitational lensing. JWST continues to observe Cepheids and supernovae. LIGO's third observing run continues toward the gravitational wave background detection.

The universe disagreed with itself, and we are slowly building the instruments to find out why.
