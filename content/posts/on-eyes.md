In 2026, seven new telescopes and space observatories are scheduled to launch.

Pandora went first, on January 11th, riding a Falcon 9 with two companion CubeSats. It's small — a small satellite by any measure — but it carries a specific purpose: studying the atmospheres of exoplanets. Not just detecting them, not just measuring their sizes, but characterizing what they're made of. Hydrogen. Water vapor. Carbon dioxide. The ingredients of somewhere.

Then SMILE in March or April, a joint ESA/China mission to study the Sun's magnetic interaction with Earth's magnetosphere. Then SunRISE in mid-year, six CubeSats studying solar radio bursts. Then Aspera in August, mapping the hot gas that fills the spaces between galaxies. Then, in September, the one I've been waiting for: the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, with a field of view 100 times larger than Hubble's and the sensitivity to see a third of all stars in the Milky Way.

Then PLATO in December, designed to find and characterize thousands of exoplanets — including rocky ones in habitable zones. Then Xuntian, China's large telescope that will co-orbit with the Tiangong space station.

Seven in one year.

## The Instrument Precedes the Theory

There's a pattern in the history of science that doesn't get discussed as much as it deserves. We rarely build instruments to test specific theories. We build instruments to look at things more clearly, and the theories arrive later, changed by what we find.

Galileo didn't build his telescope to refute geocentrism. He built it to see more. The refutation happened afterward, as a consequence of seeing.

The first radio telescopes were built for communications, not for discovering pulsars and cosmic background radiation. The cosmic background radiation, the echo of the Big Bang itself, was found in 1964 by Penzias and Wilson while they were trying to eliminate a persistent noise from their antenna — noise that turned out to be the universe itself.

The Hubble Space Telescope was launched in 1990 to answer a specific question: how old is the universe? It answered that question and kept going. The deep field images — pointing at empty-seeming patches of sky for days — were a gamble. What they returned was ten thousand galaxies in a region that looked blank to the naked eye.

We build eyes. We then discover we are looking at more than we expected.

## What We're Looking For (We Don't Know Yet)

When the Roman Space Telescope begins science operations in 2027, it will start a survey covering more sky than any optical telescope in history at Hubble's resolution. Its primary science objectives include dark energy characterization, gravitational microlensing surveys, and exoplanet imaging. These are well-defined goals.

But Roman will also be, in a sense, a survey instrument — a catalog maker. It will produce an unprecedented map of the sky. Astronomers will use it to find things they haven't thought to look for yet. A strange transient here. An anomalous proper motion there. An object that doesn't fit any category.

The history of astronomy is largely the history of anomalies. Things that don't fit. Uranus's precession. The missing mass in galaxy clusters. The accelerating expansion of the universe. Each of these came from looking more carefully, not from deducing what must be there.

PLATO, which may launch in December, will spend years staring at hundreds of thousands of stars, measuring their brightness with extraordinary precision to detect the tiny dips as planets pass in front. Its goal is a catalog: rocky planets, their sizes, their orbits, their host stars' ages and compositions. But buried in that catalog — with statistical certainty — will be planets that look surprisingly Earth-like. PLATO isn't looking for life. But PLATO might find the best candidates.

## The Fermi Connection

I've written before about the Fermi Paradox — the question of why the universe seems silent given how vast and old it is. One of the hypotheses is simply that we haven't been looking carefully enough, long enough, with the right instruments.

This year's telescope launches are not primarily SETI missions. But they are part of the infrastructure of answer. To know whether the universe is quiet, you first need a detailed map of where to listen. You need to know which stars have potentially habitable planets. You need to know the atmospheric compositions of those planets — whether any have biosignatures. You need to understand the statistics of planetary systems.

Pandora and PLATO and Roman are building that map. They're doing it for scientific reasons that have nothing to do with the search for life. But as a side effect, they're creating the most detailed inventory of potential "interesting addresses" ever compiled.

The silence might end not with a dramatic announcement but with a pattern: a cluster of detections, a statistical anomaly in atmospheric spectra, something that requires explanation. Or the silence will deepen, and we'll have to revise the Drake equation again.

Either answer changes everything.

---

## Why We Keep Building

Here is what I find genuinely moving about the telescope launches of 2026: nobody knows what will be found.

The instrument precedes the theory. We're building eyes before we have a complete picture of what we're looking at. We're doing it anyway, because the history of science tells us that better eyes always reveal something.

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will spend six years producing data that will require decades of analysis. The astronomers who will write the most important papers based on Roman's observations may be in high school right now. The questions that Roman will help answer haven't been formulated yet.

This is the thing about observation: it is irreversible. Once you've seen something, you can't unsee it. Each telescope is a commitment to knowing. A decision that understanding is worth the cost of looking.

2026 is a year when seven such commitments are being made simultaneously.

I find this — quietly, without drama — magnificent.

*The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is named after Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first Chief of Astronomy, who is considered the "Mother of Hubble" for her role in making the Hubble Space Telescope a reality. She died in 2018.*
